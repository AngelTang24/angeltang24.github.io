> (Translated from Vietnamese) When talking to parents, friends, or colleagues about an issue they genuinely care about, they will often answer essential questions such as, "But why is that?", "If so, how will this happen?", "Is it really worth doing?", "Would a normal person find this reasonable?".  
>  
> And importantly, they usually don't force themselves to find an "argument," a "mechanism," or a "counter-argument" just because those are supposed to be the elements of a debate.  
>  
>-- https://thishousedebate.com/vi/essays/cam-bay/  

So true. It's so easy to get sidetracked when you're naturally inclined to one side (Prop or Opp). The average person (you outside the debate) would probably view the point differently. In fact the average person likely has a more intuitive grasp of the circumstance. They better understand what they need to change another average person's mind. 

Also I realised debates aren't always intellectually honest or rigorous because your reasoning isn't supposed to bring the judge closer to the truth, but to make them think they are. Especially true for principle debates which relies on how emotionally resonant your rhetoric is with the judge. In other words your reasoning is supposed to bring your point closer to their intuition, rather than any enlightened truth.

So it could help if you treat it as convincing yourself. If you already have that intuition, great, now how would you reason it to the next average person? If you intuit otherwise, why? _What is needed to change another average person's mind?_
