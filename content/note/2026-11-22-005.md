Probably the most important thing in a team sport is not letting anyone feel alienated. Sure, you could say they need to prove themselves somehow someway before you can throw to them. Or maybe they’re running all the wrong places, and all you can manage in the heat of the game is a few curt lines but they still don’t learn. This is how I imagine the past seniors felt about me, and I don’t think this perspective is necessarily detrimental if you have a team of great seniors and that one confused junior.

What happens when you have to create a team from scratch? The first thing I would say is to actually care about teaching them. Not grabbing a bunch of better friends who can well play the game with you, then shooting a few lines to those juniors all over the place, expecting them to perform better the next round. That’s obviously not sufficient, but you can probably dismiss it and blame it on their lack of effort and get on with the game without improvement. They can work on each piece of feedback, but the underlying problem is the same, they haven’t played in enough matches to cultivate some sort of game sense.

So the first step can be simulating the conditions to be more beginner-friendly, ie putting people of roughly the same level and age together. They’ll learn to rise to the occasion because you’ve mitigated the risk of failing in front of way more competent seniors. But who wants to play a scuffed game? So the second step is to throw in a bunch of seniors, but make sure their role is to guide and fill in for roles some may not be ready for. Feedback should be timely, not an afterthought, and it’s good if you can make each round fulfil some kind of objective. Ie learn how to cut, learn how to run deep. Since it’s easier to do well here it’s also easier to feel satisfied with your performance, more importantly to feel you’re getting somewhere. Else why would you come to the next 3hrs of training?

The second thing is just to have more bonding. Dinners, friendly small talk, including everyone in inside jokes, so on and so forth. Just to create a more open vibe so you don’t feel immense pressure every time the disc comes zipping towards you. (In these cases more likely than not you will drop the disc.) A training session is actually sufficient to create a good team vibe, especially if you can keep a lighthearted atmosphere under the usual intense pressure of the game. From my experience you bond with those you know best on the field, but for those less participative bonding activities are a good way to break the ice.
