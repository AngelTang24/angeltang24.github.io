The last two days of SISDC 2026 have been more enjoyable than I expected. I’d missed a good debate, and this time I got to practise coming up with rebuttals on the fly. It felt less harrowing (?) than most debates, where I would be filled with a certain dread knowing I’d missed a good chunk of their content and wondering if my own substantive portion would be enough to compensate. My rebuttals also took up a measly space, even when I did tune in to their speeches, haphazardly piecing together a few dismissive lines. 

Most debates I’ve watched, even of the debaters I revere, aren’t much harder to grasp. Boiled down, they say roughly the same things we would. This also means that debates rarely need CK. At most, you need to fact-check that your information is correct or source examples. Or work backwards and get substantives there. But more often than not it’s a hindrance. Debates aren’t always intellectually honest, otherwise there is a right and wrong way for things to turn out in real life. 

So what do these debaters do differently? For one they sound a lot calmer. That could be a personal preference, and I have seen good debaters speak with utmost conviction, but for now I think it’d be better to settle for calm. I was once guilty of stuffing 8 mins’ worth of content into 6 min speeches, knowing somewhere somehow I could cut it down. But gradually I found my content more necessary, also my writing much sharper. For example Ashish Kumar speaks in a very assuring way, regularly adding tag questions (like “..right?”), as if what he were saying were the truest, most intuitive thing in the world and he were leading the judges to wisdom like sheep to a pen. 

Another thing is they cover all logical links possible. It would have never occurred to me that some things were necessary, see [Siow Xuan Ting’s speech in SISDC 2025 Open GF](https://youtu.be/qyF18k8EP8Y?t=1693). I like to write all these links down in point-form in one go, before expanding on them individually, because I’m a lot better at identifying the entire structure when I don’t delve too much into the details first. 

Amelia Tay’s set-up is strategic. Rare as the people in my club gave me the impression they did set-up to fulfill their job scope as a first speaker, not because they intended to reference it later on in their subs. I still stand by my belief (or heuristic) that you should work backwards, deriving your set-up from your substantives. Also why I used not to understand why set-up was even necessary. Couldn’t the characterisation be done in your substantives, and be labelled as such? I’ve come to think of set-up as “global variables” that bolster most of your content later on, not just a select few subs. Now I see why things like framing out <unhelpful thing> and characterisation <of the main thing> are important. I also see why you have to characterise how a narrative or norm propagates in society, things like that. It comes from doing it yourself, so I’ll try some first speaker speeches when I have the chance. 

I also had the good fortune of being adjudicated by Ashley Wee. She was very nice, or she had a very nice tone, so I liked her quite a bit. It was a rare stroke of luck, as we’d encountered an incredibly relaxed adjudicator the previous round, who then bumped our speaker scores right up the ranks. I didn’t really gain insight strategy-wise, but I got the much more important overall message that you just have to contend with your opponent’s content, i.e., rebuttals. I don’t think we’ve ever realised how important, or well-fleshed-out, rebuttals can be? Until now, my content was mostly pre-made and rebuttals added as a dismissive afterthought. Most of my speech was just waving their points out of the air and reinforcing my own, hoping the judge would do the weighing and see how much more enlightened we were. I should practise this a lot more. 

She also gave a really (in hindsight not hard to grasp) good caseline to the motion we spent 5 mins racking our heads over, before settling for a lousy caseline. It isn’t generalisable insight, but helpful enough for most social justice motions. 

So let’s check back on my goals, and see how far I’ve gotten. (I’ve got to say on a whole I’m quite proud.)

1. Add some flourish to my opening - sure, I think my openings are decent though not mindblowing. I’ve rarely seen any mindblowing intros, but I think the quality of my content makes up for it. WIP.

2. Have a few, central claims to cover in my debate - I’ll interpret this as having a succinct structure to follow. I think that’s ok. My R5 debate was a good example, I hope. (I took several contentions in the debate and explained why, while we lost in some, it was a worthy sacrifice to invest in the others - this is all very that-debate limited but I think I’m working towards it well.) There is improvement, but WIP still. 

3. Try doing everything in one logical flow. Yeah, I see improvement here as well. I don’t remember a debate where we were called out for not addressing some link? So while I can’t really announce this completed, I like the progress made here. 

These are goals I think will exist throughout my “path to enlightenment”, but I will take the courtesy of adding another (which I also mentioned a few times earlier).

4. Rebutting on the fly. Meaning, I have my substantive portion ready, but also I listen, not just listen and write, but listen, to the previous speeches. I stay calm and work through my two layers of NOT TRUE and EVEN IF. I can also work on weaving these rebuttals into my content (for me likely clashes, though I want to diversify my speaker roles), but I think I worried so much about this that I cared less about my rebuttal quality. I would say R5 was good improvement, especially for that sportswashing one. But yes I’m still roughly at square one. 

Beyond personal insight, I want to talk about team chemistry. I don’t think chemistry is hard to achieve. Everyone just needs to be willing to talk, also to treat each other as equals. The latter is important otherwise everything they do is just framed in an unfavourable light. Clarify a question. Why aren’t you up to date with the team’s direction? Contribute a point. Thanks, but what’s the point of saying that? (Followed by a dismissive hand wave, and returning to the “main” conversation.) It’s easy to feel alienated if the above is not done. I am prone to looking down on some points. Then again because of some bias I’m as prone to greatly trusting my own, so I should probably take both instincts with a pinch of salt. 

One way to explain this bias is that in my head, I’ve roughly reasoned to why my point is true, even if I don’t mention it. Also I’ve probably referred to past examples or experiences to bolster this intuition. Whereas for others, unless immediately obvious, I struggle to see its merit immediately. 

But true enough, some people may just churn out more (in hopefully no critical or condescending way) useless points. They raise it up more as questions, and that uncertainty makes their point more vulnerable, and more “accepting” of attacks. So we attack it, even though to other similarly bad points we would just clarify what they intended to do with it. Here I’m not talking about anyone’s actual debating skill, that is whatever work you need to do on your own. But if you want to be included in the conversation, you should feel part of the conversation. You are, rightfully so. 

I think that also goes towards contributing to the caseline. Just being willing to talk, to spit out ideas, not to patiently wait for your substantive portion to be delegated to you. That’s what I feel slightly hostile towards, but I should change that too. Talking contributes to the team’s overall vibe, the welcome familiarity you feel when you go to different tournaments with that same group of people. It also gives meaning to debate “as a team sport”, otherwise half the time you’re writing your own speech, secretly hoping to clinch a speaker award, nevermind if you break. 

I hear a lot of people in the club saying they joined because they liked to argue. They found pushing their own stance with reason and rhetoric refreshing, as well as deeply satisfying when they won. Yet the same people never interact during debate sessions, settling for a half-hearted speech. Of course it’s going to be half-hearted, you’re not arguing with someone you want to convince, nor are you even sure your arguments are being heard by anyone, other than for point of rebuttal. The passion in their arguments is snuffed out when introduced to a rigid debating format. 

The most important thing is to fervently believe that what you’re saying is correct. This is probably harder to do in an informal setting, where both sides just want to go home (after club sessions) and don’t really care about what they’re saying, or what they think of the motion.You can’t mimic conviction. You can’t go up and act like what you’re saying is intuitive, if you yourself don’t believe it is. That’s kind of where talking with the team comes in. If you pass ideas back and forth, if these ideas are challenged, when you get the team in on your ideas, polish them into stone, that’s when you feel conviction. Essentially, your team hypes you up. Your team is the one carrying the burden with you, but when all three speakers give bits and tatters of an argument I mean of course it’s hard not to feel you’re saying something worthless. 

This also means you don’t let bits of content you’re unsure about remain in your speech. This also means not settling for mediocre rebuttals. This is a really high bar, considering the frenzy in the speech leading up to yours. But if you can refine your quality to satisfaction, then you’re going to feel a lot more assured when presenting your content. That helps. 

You could really just do it for the love of the game. But all these things above are what keep you going, fuel for a steam engine.
