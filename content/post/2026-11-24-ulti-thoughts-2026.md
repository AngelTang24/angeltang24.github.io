> Sport is supposed to be something majestic and enthralling and gruelling and heartbreaking but I can't seem to put myself out there enough to achieve any of this.

Notice how true this is for any sport; the swiftness and grace of techniques players refine for years on end. The disc cruising against a pink and purple sky -- you can imagine the appeal of the sport to an enthralled sideline. I wanted my throws to look as assured, movement as instinctive, wanted to feel the certainty of the disc in my hand. I haven't exactly achieved that, not as a handler, but now when I receive the disc adrenaline pushes me forward, instead of the contending fear that makes my heart race. 

## Last season (25/26)

I'd invested into ulti uncertainly. Most of the time I was carried by the flow, in that I knew I had signed up for the team and was obligated to carry out my duties as such. I wasn't horrible at ulti, I scored a few compliments at some trainings, but overall my contribution was minimal. I was one of those juniors they let in "for sake of continuity", instead of being any real prodigy (which my friends, to my delight and chagrin, assumed to be the case). 

I guess I started off the wrong footings. I could never quite grasp the terminology--break side, open side, OTL[^1], PMB, slider, upfield--and learnt the ropes mostly by experience. Instead of thinking, "Ah, at the sidelines you engage OTL," it was mostly, "Ah, that's a strike. I wonder why they ran a strike, but it does look clean." 

There were trainings that paid off. Those were in the evening, when the air was cool and you could feel the swiftness of movement cutting through a block of wind. I remember best one training where I was on zone D, specifically the cup. My job was easy, to block the swings between the handlers. And so I did it well, each minute driving up my confidence and fuelling the next. It helped that I felt a part of the team now, no longer just an uncertain pawn lingering at the back[^2]. 

I also remember trainings under the morning sun. Those were horrible, probably because of the sweltering heat. Each break gave no respite, only a dread to the next 30mins of play. Even worse was when we conducted drills and I had to wait in line, wondering with very real concern if my brains would be fried to a crisp. 

But all of this isn't what mainly held it back from being enjoyable. There were a lot of evening trainings where I felt unremarkable on the field, even by my very low standards. I knew that I could probably afford to initiate more cuts, and it wasn't that I didn't know how to run a strategic one. It was easy to spot when the congestion cleared and the handler was looking upfield for someone, anyone to cut; those were the moments that came easily to me. So when I did cut, I did so well.

But the game wasn't as simple as that.  Many people were initiating some form of movement, and oftentimes the disc sailed towards them before I could contribute. I didn't know how to look ahead, only to look at the players all over the place in despair. Most games quickly strayed from the conventional Vert Stack I knew best, and with so many options for the handler, my contributions came to a standstill. I was never bold enough to run deep, even if I were there would be someone at my place already. So I was flustered. I could only pull off a good cut, in the simplest way possible: run into open space. 

You can imagine my partial relief when it was announced that we wouldn't be playing IJC. There was some disappointment, but also I knew that if trainings were already this confusing, actual matches would be a lot more so. I couldn't afford to feel useless in an actual match, not with spectators following your every move and opponents easily ten times more athletic. We could win by well-calculated strategy and teamwork, but I didn't feel like a cohesive addition to the team, didn't feel ready to be called from the sidelines onto the field. I'd imagined the disappointment when they would inevitably realise they had recruited a clueless, but well-meaning junior to a tournament as high stakes as IJC. 

This were all signs that I was doing something wrong. Everyone was ultra hyped up, I guess I can partly blame it on them being a lot closer in age and trusting each other more, but they were ultimately united by a love for the sport. Everyone seemed up and ready to play at IJC, anxiety only a side product of their bubbly excitement. Their anxiety looked very similar to mine, except that I was mostly convinced there was no world where I would play stellarly. Them, on the other hand, had far better prospects, even logging in onto some application where they could check their number of assists and goals. 

What held me back? A number of factors. 
1. Feeling alienated from the team. I don't think M, the other junior was in the team with me, felt this way. She'd been in the season before this one and seemed to be more well-acquainted with the seniors. This is by no means any of the senior' fault, but practical reasons like age and skill discouraged me from trying to mingle. I still don't know how I would have, without first upping my game. Problem is a lot of believing yourself also believing that your senior believes you, and not feeling useless on the team. 

2. I just couldn't get my skills right. I was familiar, sure, with a certain game strategy our coach had laid out for us. I hard-memorised[^3] the handler movement, but a cutter didn't play a big part in a handler-centric strategy. Other than that, my throwing wasn't good, I could manage a clean forehand and backhand outside the stress of the game, but that was largely it. Here's a line from before:  

> I grip the disc in my hand and it wobbles mockingly, so I release it and let out the breath I didn't know I'd been holding. 

3. I didn't put myself out there a lot. This is probably a result of the first factor, but significant enough I should mention it. A lot of times I was waiting for a more competent senior to come in and get the disc, and oftentimes they did. I didn't know how well-received my cuts would be, nor did I want to jeopardise my already precarious position in the team. I thought it would be better to be mediocre than to be bad, especially in a setting where I could be easily compared to way better players. They knew what they were doing, so my intervention could mess it up. It wouldn't be a harmless effort but probably interfere with the cohesiveness of movement, so I just settled for being mediocre. No one really pointed this out, so I uncomfortably eased myself into a pawn's role. 

> I don't feel the tingling sensation in my arm when the disc flies at rather rather a heart racing trepidation that it'll crash right into me and I'll fumble it and I'll be rubbing at my sore chest while recovering from a turnover. 

I'm making my experience sound dreadful. For a good ~2 years it was a mediocre experience, I can't say I didn't have my handful of good times, but I didn't know what I was doing right. I couldn't bring that confidence to every training session because there was still something fundamentally wrong with my understanding of the sport, and it was holding me back. I didn't sign up with high hopes, nor a belief that if I worked at it hard enough I would get something out of it. My moments to shine were brief and unprecedented, but easily dimmed down by stellar players who knew the game inside out. It was hard, after a while, to still put myself out there when they would surely come in, flawlessly sustaining the momentum of the game. The game didn't need me. 

These were the things that largely defined my experience in the first half of the year. I carried these impressions into the second half as well, which also explains why I was reluctant to re-join the team. Not to mention I just didn't feel like investing that time anymore, after a turbulent June holidays[^4]. 

## This season (26/27)

This season didn't start off amazing. I didn't expect it to, but it kept me active and the evenings were a nice time to spend with my friends. There was also the upcoming Games Day (though scuffed). 

I still felt left out from trainings. Although most of the seniors from the previous season had left, there was an influx of y4s (one year above me) into the sport. There is a bit of a personal grudge here, I didn't like how they were, outspoken and overly brash. They also seemed to have their own tight-knit circle, which also played out during games. They trusted those in that circle and only them, which made me feel I was running around like a headless chicken, squeezing in a few good cuts but getting no recognition. I didn't like their humour, so I always opted out of post-training dinners. 

So a feeling I knew all too well came back. 

> But I'm growing alongside all the seniors. When I was y1 they were y3 and I admired them endlessly; now I'm y3 and they're y5 and it sure looks like it'll forever be this dynamic: me admiring the sport from the sidelines whereas on the field I'm the same blundering mess I was. I see juniors start to trickle into the sport and I'm starting to think it's a you-get-it or you-don't sport. It's supposed to be excitement that sets in whenever someone calls for a scrimmage, and not going along with it as part of training. 

This was my reflection from quite recent, 13 Sept. I remember being quite dejected that training: crouching beside a more optimistic newcomer on the field, as always admiring the sport from the sidelines. I can't exactly remember why I was on the bench, but I remembered being some part relieved as well, but also furious at myself for not making something out of the hours I'd put into the sport.

## What changed

Then during the holidays (the time I'm writing this), something changed. For one, these y4s no longer committed to the sport. They were out of the picture[^5]. My throwing also improved, though I wasn't significantly more reliable than before. My grip felt a lot more certain on the disc, and yes it started looking a lot more like what the seniors did[^6]. I also had a friend join, new to the game, so as part of my job to show her the ropes I had to seem a lot more reliable than I felt. This helped, funnily enough, as now I had to show her what a good cut looked like, and so I was a lot more willing to cut. 

My performance was the slightest bit improving, but I can't pinpoint the time it started going up. If I were to try, I would say when J started coaching us. He dragged a more amateurish team (which I'm chagrined to say included me) aside, laying out the most barebones objectives of the game and getting us to run them over and over again. It was easy to get out a good cut, now that everyone was going to their proper places as I'd learnt originally. He integrated more elements of play, taking it a notch higher but making sure we could follow. While most of even the amateurish group knew this already, this was the time for us to really work out our fundamentals. _How else_, he reasoned, _would we be able to incorporate these things into an actual, more complex game?_

This also goes with what I mentioned in [this note](/note/2026/11/note-5/):

> Feedback should be timely, not an afterthought, and it’s good if you can make each round fulfil some kind of objective. Ie learn how to cut, learn how to run deep. Since it’s easier to do well here it’s also easier to feel satisfied with your performance, more importantly to feel you’re getting somewhere. 

From then on, I think I felt a lot more comfortable playing. Slowly, I started to see how those fundamentals played out in a more complex game. I didn't realise that it'd worked its way into my instincts until quite recent, when running strikes and cutting deep swiftly followed the movement of the disc. It helped that the people I was playing with were not that far above my skill level, so I felt like we were working through this together, instead of me still having a long way to catch up. 

So I put myself out there a lot more. I tried hard during drills, and to my pleasure it worked out well. I now understood, from experience, what people meant with OTL, PMB, pre-cuts, etc. I saw how seniors incorporated those drills into actual gameplay. No longer did their cuts feel flawless or unreplicable, instead it felt like everyone was going all out for the game, and it was only right if everyone on the team did so. Sure, a few cuts felt a lot more better timed, but those I could work my way towards.

And so last training (I think, at the time of writing) I felt all this come together. J came back to coach and the team worked together well, promptly clearing out of open space and cutting when it was their turn[^7]. Cutting felt natural now. It only made sense to me to cut at so and so timing, when the lane was open and handler looking upfield. It wasn't rocket science and it hadn't been, except now the team I was playing with made it a lot easier to facilitate. So when he complimented me for a "good game sense", I took it with an easy rush of pleasure but also knew I had to work on cases where it wouldn't be that easy to cut. 

## What I hope to sustain 

And so that's now. Now, I think I see open spaces even when we're not strictly in a stack, and strategic movement comes a lot easier to me. None of it, if you were to watch the game, is actually rocket science. Something just clicked in me, allowing me to put myself out there a lot more, and trust that I could receive the disc between two firm hands. Now I feel the game needs me. 

Here are the following thing I hope to sustain:
1. Continuing to identify open spaces, and timing my cuts well. When I see the disc flying near the endzone, I should run to the endzone to make myself open. When I cut and I'm not open, that means the momentum of my defender is with me and I can probably make a deep cut open. I should always try to provide options for the handler without clogging up the space. It's a very dynamic game, and with experience I should be able to refine my game sense.

2. Bonding with the team. I like the team I'm with now, and I think our chemistry is good. After each point we talk lightheartedly, and that probably helps our gameplay to a non-negligible extent. I go for dinners a lot more often now. We also coordinate our movement into different spaces well, preventing double-cuts or congestion in the open lane. Most of the time if I'm not running towards the end zone, someone else is there ready to receive the disc. 

3. Being more aware of what's going on behind me as a D. It's easy to predict where the power position will go, and D-ing is less intimidating when you can use your body to block off that path. Sometimes the handler will also signal for my mark to go a certain way, which I can reliably cut off if I look back. Basically predicting their movement beforehand, so I can be a more effective D. Learning the fundamental concepts helps with this as well. 

4. I don't think I'm doing this right now, but I probably should. Making pre-cuts. So far I've been able to break my mark more often than not, but in IJC I don't think this will be the case. Also now I don't really think about breaking my mark. I cut, and if I don't happen to be open, I just clear. I think that's a good practice, as cuts I find natural I can carry out without worrying my D will block off. 

And that's about it. I'm now surrounded by people I'm more familiar with, so my instincts kick in faster and I'm less afraid of muddling up whatever system I'm not involved in. Steady progress is a great confidence booster when you've been stuck for so long, and I hope to invest more into the sport now that I've been getting some return out of it.
