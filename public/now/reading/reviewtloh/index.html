Review: The Lathe of Heaven

Last edited 2 November 2026

War rages and global warming wrecks havoc on the quality of life everywhere as seven billion people jostle for living space and food. For George Orr, a mild and unremarkable man, the world is overwhelmingly difficult. But George is different: his dreams can change reality – although he has no means of controlling this extraordinary power. Psychiatrist Dr William Haber offers to help, directing George to dream a world without racism. But as ambition gets the better of ethics, no one can predict the devastating consequences.

The progression of events can be summed up as monkey’s paw after monkey’s paw, except Haber never learns his lesson and is convinced that each step forward is progress for humanity.

Reading the prose itself was a pleasure. Le Guin also threw in seemingly random descriptions of the characters then referenced them later on as part of an altered reality, which kept the story exciting even if the premise was clear from the beginning. The jargon interspersed throughout the text helped more than it hindered, because it gave dreaming a mystic, supernatural effect rather than being outright magic, keeping it from being reduced to repeated manifestations of the monkey’s paw. I appreciate how she didn’t beat around the bush and got the story going from the very first page.

Now to the plot. There is a lot of focus on the “continuum”, which is the fabric of space, time and memory that is overhauled and restitched every time Orr “effectively” dreams. Orr detests Haber playing god, whereas Haber believes it is one’s grander purpose to harness the tools at hand for the greater good.

“But in fact, isn’t that man’s very purpose on earth — to do things, change things, run things, make a better world?”

“No!”

“What is his purpose, then?”

“I don’t know. Things don’t have purposes, as if the universe were a machine, where every part has a useful function. What’s the function of a galaxy? I don’t know if our life has a purpose and I don’t see that it matters. What does matter is that we’re a part. Like a thread in a cloth or a grass-blade in a field. It is and we are. What we do is like wind blowing on the grass.”

Orr’s passive, laid-back character is mentioned quite a few times. Once he laments why such a power was bestowed upon him, weak-willed and defeatist as he is.

Why had this gift been given to a fool, a passive nothing of a man? Why was Orr so sure and so right, while the strong, active, positive man was powerless, forced to try to use, even to obey, the weak tool?

He suggests that Haber might be more suited for the job, and indeed Haber decides that’s not too bad an idea after all and harnesses his own unconscious mind using the Augmentor. However Haber’s “effective” dreams disintegrate all the realities created by Orr, and coherent existence dissolves (described quite literally - “like jello left out in the sun”) into chaos.

There’s also some speculation on whether others could possess this ability.

“Did you ever happen to think, Dr. Haber,” he said, quietly enough but stuttering a little, “that there, there might be other people who dream the way I do? That reality’s being changed out from under us, replaced, renewed, all the time — only we don’t know it? Only the dreamer knows it, and those who know his dream.

This suggests that a continuum is constantly overhauled and re-established by the will of many unconscious minds.

“…Everything dreams. The play of form, of being, is the dreaming of substance. Rocks have their dreams, and the earth changes….But when the mind becomes conscious, when the rate of evolution speeds up, then you have to be careful…A conscious mind must be part of the whole, intentionally and carefully — as the rock is part of the whole unconsciously.”

So not just other living, breathing beings then! The last sentence suggests that the rock, having no conscious mind to shape its personality, can alter the world while maintaining harmony with it. We, owning a conscious mind, on the other hand, must take heed not to let our personalities and inclination to rationalise possibly unknowable concepts interfere with the way of the universe.

Orr comes along and fulfills these criteria. Stunningly so, as pointed out in the results of standardised testing. He is, as Haber describes, “the man in the middle of the graph”. His extraversion/introversion score was a 49.1, dominance a 48.8, similarly so for creative/destructive, the Ramirez scale. Haber sees it as “

\[cancelling\]

out so thoroughly that, in a sense, nothing is left”. In other words, he sees Orr’s lack of inclination towards any belief or personality trait as weak-willed, submissive. This directly challenges his belief that “life - evolution - the whole universe of space/time, matter/energy - existence itself - is essentially change”. Compare this to Walters, another guy down at Med School, who sees Orr’s results as a “peculiar state of poise, of self-harmony”. He turns out to be right.

So why was Haber’s intervention the tipping point? My take is that Orr’s passivity makes his unconscious mind sufficiently permissive to alter reality and still maintain a cohesive world. Whereas Haber, driven by hubris and, more importantly, the persistent belief that he could mold the universe to an ideal form, caused the universe to “lash” back and restore the heavenly equilibrium. So the character contrast was necessary to explain why, when Haber took the reins, all the realities constructed by Orr, just barely yielding to the “lathe of heaven”, gave way and disintegrated into undifferentiated chaos.

To let understanding stop at what cannot be understood is a high attainment. Those who cannot do it will be destroyed on the lathe of heaven.

This is the claim that striving to attain something fundamentally unknowable will fight the natural, unalterable balance of the universe and lead to your undoing.

Did you know that “the lathe of heaven” was an inaccurate translation of the original text? “天鈞” is better described as heavenly equilibrium, which doesn’t really interfere with the essential idea. But “lathe” – a machine that grinds raw material down – suggests that the ensuing chaos was definitive and inevitable, whereas “鈞”, a potter’s wheel, is gentler. The latter translation would better fit Orr’s metaphor of us being grass blades in a field, and the cosmic universe the wind that blows and sways us. Anyhow, I suppose “lathe” better contends with how Haber was trying to utilise the unconscious mind in a clinical and persistent manner.

So it’s interesting to see how the medium - the dream, the unconscious mind, the underlying personalities that drive it - ties back to the concept of “the lathe of heaven”. I do think there’s more to look into for the dreaming aspect – the aspect that drew me in from the blurb.

On the irrationality of the unconscious mind:

“Don’t be afraid of your unconscious mind! It’s not a black pit of nightmares. Nothing of the kind! It is the wellspring of health, imagination, creativity.

This goes hand in hand with Haber’s entire positivist outlook. He dismisses the idea that anything bad might spring out of Orr’s dreams, even as Orr tells him that compelling or commanding an inherently irrational side of him to produce good results will never work out.

Your own ideas are sane and rational, but this is my unconscious you’re trying to use, not my rational mind…You’re trying to reach progressive, humanitarian goals with a tool that isn’t suited to the job. Who has humanitarian dreams?

That about makes sense. I’d always been intrigued by what the unconscious mind symbolises. Is it just this supernatural realm we’re not supposed to meddle in? There must be some reason that the brain retreats there for good intervals of 30mins every night, before we awake in our conscious self and our memories of the previous night are wiped clean.

This ties back in nicely to the quote on unknowable knowledge. I guess the claim is that we must not attempt to harness or rationalise the irrational, as if it were a machine we could compel to churn out good results. Our understanding necessarily stops at our unconscious self, and any effort to “

\[discover\]

and

\[control\]

” can only backfire.

Sure enough, it proves terrible for the job.

“That’s what happened with the Child Centers. I suggested that the nuclear family being the prime shaper of neurotic personality structures, there were certain ways in which it might, in an ideal society, be modified. Your dream simply grabbed at the crudest interpretation of these, mixed it up with cheap utopian concepts, or cynical anti-utopian concepts perhaps, and produced the Centers.”

I don’t know if this can be generalised to all dreams (that dreams tend to take shortcuts and work their way around instructions) or if it hints, further, at Orr’s character. Orr’s will to stick with the flow, thus crippling his “effective” dreaming capacities and causing Haber’s hypnotic suggestions to be “twisted out of all recognition”. In other words, for Orr’s implementation of Haber’s suggestions to not interfere with heavenly equilibrium, there must be a counteracting force (for example, the aliens).

Also, perhaps because I’ve always been intrigued by dreaming and the unconscious mind, there’s a very Inception-y feel to it. I’m glad the story didn’t end with Orr waking up in a dream within a dream within a dream…but throughout he does question how much of it can be considered objective reality.

On Friday

\[Orr\]

had been going all to pieces over a mere ethical point; here on Wednesday in the midst of Armageddon he was cool and calm…Or did he think, Haber suddenly wondered, that because he had dreamed the invasion, it was all just a dream?

At the beginning of the novel, he was dying among radioactive ruins, one of the survivors of a nuclear holocaust but eventually to be wiped out. He dreams a somewhat better world, and so he wakes up in one. As his ability reaches Haber’s hands and he demands more “effective dreams,” Orr has to reconcile many more simultaneous timelines. I won’t bore you with each; there’s a neat timeline attached below if you’re interested.

In the end it coalesces into one. It’s a kaleidoscopic world where grey is just another race among many (so there’s still a chance with Heather!), the turtle-like aliens are here to stay, and the population density remains roughly the same but is managed better. I don’t think, though, that Orr really grapples with the objectivity of reality if he can dream up a new one every night, rather he finds a lot more problems with it on a moral basis. It remains interesting to think about.

Finally, on the morality of Haber’s actions. I think the clash between his and Orr’s mentalities comes to light when Haber raises the thought experiment of him encountering a woman who has been bitten by a snake.

“…Do you withhold it because ‘this is the way it is’ — do you ‘let her be’?”

I don’t think Orr gives a very satisfactory answer. He struggles a little with this thought experiment, because it does seem to prove a need for intervention. He tells Haber that “he doesn’t know whether

\[he’s\]

doing good or evil or both”. He eventually reasons a better answer:

But it’s not right to play God with masses of people. To be God you have to know what you’re doing.…You have to…be in touch. He isn’t in touch. No one else, no thing even, has an existence of its now, for him; he seems the world only as means to his end.

I think that’s quite revelatory, right? It kind of touches down on the idea of respecting human dignity on its own, rather than using humans as a means to an end of creating a better world - this kind of sounds like the argument against utilitarianism, doesn’t it? A doctor comes in and decides whether or not he should proactively sacrifice that one person to save five others…a man stands atop a bridge and wonders if he should push the man in front of him onto the tracks…why is something considered immoral if it creates a more fortunate world? Here the benefits are amplified - there is no need to ration, no need to worry about the masses suffering from kwashiorkor. I guess it also helps that wiping out six million people feels less proximate to us when it’s presented as a click-and-snap kind of action.

No respect for the present. I think that’s a solid answer. Which is also why he’s out of touch, out of touch with the present, out of touch with the masses of people he’s supposed to bring greater happiness to, except he’s willing to forgo them all to progress a more ideal version of humanity. Greater happiness of the population is supposed to be his objective, everything else only instrumental, but it becomes the case where the people are secondary to them.

These are two black and white areas, though. If you were to reject Orr’s go-with-the-flow mentality, and also reject Haber’s persistent positivity, you would still have a lot of grey area to work with. How best to then determine their preferences and progress towards a better world? I guess you could take the shortcut and say respect for their dignity is an inalienable principle. But it doesn’t really deal with the elephant in the room, right? Masses of people - survivors, ironically, of a nuclear holocaust - are doomed to die a slow death from radiation. Could you contend that whatever world Orr dreamt up would have been less ideal? Orr himself, reluctantly or not, admits to that.

But then what gives us the prerogative to meddle with such matters, assuming the capability to do so? I am reminded of the slippery slope argument: even if in this one-off instance it might be better to meddle, you blur the line and increasingly permit agents to meddle in even more matters. I can imagine Haber thinking the same thing - if it were right to stop a nuclear threat, why isn’t it right to stop kwashiorkor? Why isn’t it right to stop wars? Racism, climate change? Disregarding that the heavenly equilibrium will act through some other force (for example the aliens), why? The principle backing rule utilitarianism doesn’t seem to work here, arguably everything done seems to be as justified as the next.

Of course, again this is all a very click-and-snap sort of ideal. We can’t solve these issues at the wave of our hand, but those we can do manifest in smaller, less morally grey settings. There, it’s been roughly established that every single instance you act in an assumedly more optimised way, you also broaden the prerogative to do so in more unjustified ways. Which is also why administering the antidote to the woman with the snakebite is ok to intervene in, because I’m in touch with what she needs, and every in-touch act of helping others is likely to result in more good.

But to answer the question, I think we can only find a comfortable middle ground between the two ends. I don’t think there’s a one-size-fits-all answer, but I would say it’s safer than not to always abide by respect for human dignity. To recognise us in the present, as the book says, instead of serving as means towards an end. In the story arguably quite a noble end, but we see that there’s no quite fairytale ending to any of the problems Haber tries to resolve, and besides that there are an infinitude of problems to pick and solve! There’s a long list of instrumental goals you can work towards resolving but you can’t forgo the people in the present, their dignity and their happiness. There’s a mixture of deontological and utilitarian benefit to be weighed and considered here, I think it’s truly interesting how they contend in the story, though Le Guin probably wants to convey more of Taoist principles. But we got that aside in the first half of this review, and now under the assumption we are able to meddle with the universe without causing its collapse, what is our best course of action?

This book is one that leaves readers with many questions. But it’s so gratifying to answer them all, because they tie in really nicely with the different elements of the story. I don’t think I fully realised all this while reading, but I’m glad to have reconciled my thoughts in this review.

“To a better world!” Dr. Haber said, raising his glass to his creation, and finished his whisky in a lingering, savouring swallow.
